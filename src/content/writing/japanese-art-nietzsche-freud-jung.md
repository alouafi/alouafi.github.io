I’ve always been a sucker for “edgy” and “pretentious” stories. Give me a tormented protagonist, some obscure religious imagery, and a plot that questions God or reality, and I’m in. That is more or less how I ended up with _Xenogears_ (that, and my obsession with Japan and RPGs, I guess).

I knew the game by reputation long before I played it. It was the one people described as too ambitious for its own budget: a second disc told mostly through narrated slides because the team ran out of time, and a final boss literally named after God. That was enough to sell me.

The game did not teach me philosophy. Most of it went straight over my head. I knew nothing about repression or the Jungian Shadow, and the quote-unquote “death of God” sounded like a strange paradox and not much more. But it left me with questions about memory, desire, guilt, and the need for things to mean something, and I had no vocabulary for any of them.

I only started reading Nietzsche, Freud, and Jung seriously during my year at the University of Tokyo, which in hindsight is a funny place to discover European philosophy. I kept getting a weird feeling of “anamnesis”. I was reading these ideas for the first time, and yet they felt familiar. Japanese fiction had shown them to me years before I knew their names.

I doubt I’m the only one. For many people, Nietzsche first appeared as a God they had to fight at the end of an RPG, years before they opened _Thus Spoke Zarathustra_. Freud arrived as a monstrous doppelgänger or a buried memory. Jung was hiding behind a mask, a Shadow, or a persona.

These supposedly “difficult” ideas are everywhere in Japanese video games, manga, and anime, and the same figures keep coming back: the tortured teenager, the artificial god, the social mask, the falsified memory, the end of the world. A fascination for Western culture explains part of it. Japan’s own history and traumas explain what happens to these borrowed ideas once they arrive.

## A philosophy learned through images

Western philosophy has been studied in Japan since the 明治 (Meiji) era. Nietzsche, Freud, and Jung had long been translated and discussed in universities and intellectual circles there. Thinkers such as 西田幾多郎 (Nishida Kitarō) and the 京都学派 (Kyōto-gakuha, Kyoto School) tried to connect European philosophy with Buddhism and Japanese traditions.

In Japanese high schools, there is a special course called 倫理 (rinri), usually translated as “ethics”, which introduces the major thinkers and their doctrines. It has much less impact and importance than philosophy does in a French lycée. Students generally learn concepts and authors instead of actually building a dissertation or applying dialectics.

Students are expected to memorize “God is dead” without ever sitting with what it would mean for every value that structures their life to disappear. You can learn that Jung theorized the Shadow without once asking what yours looks like.

Fiction is where these ideas get concrete. A teacher can probably give you a somewhat clear definition of the unconscious. _Silent Hill 2_ builds an entire town from the guilt and denial of its protagonist and makes that concept feel real. A textbook can explain the difference between the social self and intimate desires. _Perfect Blue_ shows an identity breaking apart under the public gaze.

Video games go one step further and make the player act inside the concept. A book can ask what you think of an idea. A game can drop you into a world built on that idea and ask what you are going to do there.

The route from universities to popular culture also passed through the student movements of the 1960s and 1970s, the New Left, science fiction, experimental cinema, and a growing critique of institutions. Freud, Marx, Nietzsche, and existentialism gave Japanese creators a way to question the family, the state, work, and official narratives. These ideas left the libraries and entered manga, films, television, and eventually video games.

## 1945: living after the sky fell

My guess is that much of this obsession goes back to the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the surrender, the American occupation, and the transformation of institutions. Together, they broke a political system and, more importantly, a symbolic order and a whole system of belief. Imperial power had stood at the center of a sacred vision of the nation. In its own way, this was another “God is dead”. In January 1946, Emperor Hirohito’s so-called “Humanity Declaration” separated the emperor from this official conception, although historians still debate the exact meaning of the text and of imperial divinity.

European Christianity and the Japanese imperial system have very different histories. Still, the comparison with Nietzsche gets at something they share: living in a world where the highest authority can no longer guarantee the values that gave people a sense of purpose.

For Nietzsche, the death of God could be as much a catastrophe as a blessing in disguise. Once the transcendent foundation of good and truth disappears, humanity has to invent its own values, without any certainty of succeeding.

Japan after the war also had to rebuild a collective horizon. Economic growth, technology, the company, and consumption offered new narratives. Yukio Mishima took this crisis in a nationalist, extreme direction. He saw postwar comfort as spiritual emptiness, returned in his books to the body, beauty, death and the emperor, then attempted a coup and killed himself in 1970. The destroyed country became an industrial power, while the trauma stayed beneath the surface.

One of the earliest depictions of that trauma, and still one of the best, is _Godzilla_ (1954). Awakened and contaminated by nuclear power, the creature destroys a city that still carries the memory of the bombings. _Akira_ returns to this image. Tokyo has been destroyed and rebuilt as a shining metropolis, but underneath remain laboratories, sacrificed children, and the seeds of another apocalypse.

_Neon Genesis Evangelion_ places this anxiety inside the body. The Evas are organic beings controlled by adults whose plans go far beyond their teenage pilots. The Human Instrumentality Project promises to abolish loneliness by merging every consciousness. This form of salvation requires individuality to disappear. Should we end the separation between people if it also means losing the possibility of being an individual altogether?

Of everything in this essay, the ending of _Evangelion_ is the one that stayed with me the longest. After all that running away, Shinji is offered a world where nobody can ever hurt him again, and he turns it down. He chooses separate bodies, and other people who can reject him. It struck me as oddly hopeful for such a bleak story, though I could not have explained why. Reading Nietzsche later gave me part of the answer: refusing an easy salvation and accepting a life with no guarantee looks a lot like what he asked of us.

In _Xenogears_, religions, institutions, and historical narratives hide systems of domination. Even divinity eventually reveals its material and constructed nature. Fei has to refuse the identities that others constructed for him, and the game remains dubious about systems that promise any absolute form of salvation. What can still guide a life once gods, parents, and institutions have failed?

## From inherited traumas to the crisis of the 1990s

_Evangelion_, _Persona_, and _Xenogears_ appeared decades after the war, mostly in the Japan of the 1990s. The economic bubble had collapsed and the “Lost Decade” had begun. The model based on growth, stable employment, and integration into the company started to crack. In 1995, the Kobe earthquake and the sarin gas attack by オウム真理教 (Ōmu Shinrikyō) made the feeling of insecurity even stronger.

The postwar generation had replaced the old order with a promise of prosperity. The next generation found out that this promise was fragile too. Its heroes were teenagers asked to save a world that adults had already made uninhabitable. Shinji has to get into the Eva. Fei has to confront a history he never chose, passed down through generations. The protagonists of _Persona_ repair the corrupted desires of a society that still claims to teach them how to become adults. Their breakdowns are personal, but they also tell you a whole social model has run out of steam.

_Texhnolyze_ (2003) pushes this further. I read Kazuho Yoshii as a distorted Übermensch: he descends into a city that has accepted its own death and tries to wake it up by imposing his will through conflict, and only speeds up its collapse.

## The mask as a compromise

The notions of 本音 (honne) and 建前 (tatemae) often appear when people discuss the tension between the individual and society in Japan, albeit sometimes to a caricatural extent. 本音 (honne) refers to intimate feelings and desires. 建前 (tatemae) is the behavior adapted to social expectations.

Every society separates what people feel from what they show, of course, and in France we are quick to point at the Japanese for something we do all the time. The social mask is ambiguous. It protects privacy and limits conflict, and it may be part of what makes living together possible at all. Jung called this public face the persona (and there is your aha moment for why the video game series is called that). It becomes dangerous when a person starts confusing it with the whole self. Behind it remains the Shadow, made of the desires, fears, and possibilities we refuse to recognize as ours.

The _Persona_ games turn this model directly into a narrative and gameplay system. In _Persona 4_, denying your Shadow makes it stronger. A character progresses by accepting that the Shadow belongs to them, even when its words are distorted or cruel. In _Persona 5_, the heroes tear off masks imposed by adults who define their place. They then use these same masks to give a form and a language to their revolt.

In _Perfect Blue_, Mima leaves her career as an idol to become an actress, but the public keeps demanding her former image. That image turns into a double that stalks her. Soon she cannot tell if she really wants her new life, or if every version of herself was made by other people looking at her. Satoshi Kon does not resolve it, and I’m still not sure an authentic self can survive once images have shaped every way we see ourselves.

## When what is buried takes on a body

Freud gives another language to this crisis. For him, the repressed never completely disappears. It gets buried and comes back as symptoms, dreams, repetitions, or behaviors whose origin stays hidden from the person.

Japanese pop culture loves to give this return a physical form. In _Xenogears_, a character named Id gives a body to the violence tied to Fei’s trauma. His name directly evokes the Freudian id (although, from what I remember, the character is a bit more subtle than just that).

In _Silent Hill 2_, the whole town works that way. The creatures give a form to James’s guilt, desires, and buried memories. To move forward, he has to come closer to a truth that he has organized his whole existence to avoid.

In _Paprika_, a machine opens dreams and lets the unconscious contaminate reality. _Paranoia Agent_ turns a similar mechanism into a social phenomenon. A fantasy spreads because an entire society needs an external culprit. In both works, private fears escape the individual and become collective.

## Playing the unconscious

Video games can go further because they place these conflicts inside their rules. In _Persona_, the player divides their time between school, social relationships, and psychic worlds. The friendships built after school make you stronger in combat, so the game never lets you treat social life and inner life as separate.

_NieR: Automata_ uses repetition in a world where creators, causes, and purposes have disappeared. The player still has to act. Replaying events from another perspective slowly destroys the certainty that we understood what we were doing in the first place. Enemies become subjects. Missions lose their legitimacy. Meanwhile, the machines imitate human behavior because they are desperately searching for a reason to exist.

Pascal, Simone de Beauvoir, and Kierkegaard show up as machine names, often with obvious irony. Their doctrines survive as fragments in a world that has forgotten what to do with them. Elsewhere, Nietzsche, Freud, and Jung become mythological material alongside Kabbalah, Christianity, Buddhism, Shinto, and Gnosticism. Japanese fiction absorbs all of it and produces something of its own.

## A collective couch, beyond Japan

I like the idea of Japanese pop culture as a “collective couch”, but it has its limits. Japanese society is obviously too diverse to receive one diagnosis. These stories come from different creators and periods, and their audiences do not all read them in the same way.

Still, many of them share a background: the memory of the war, nuclear fear, technological reconstruction, institutional pressure, economic crisis, and isolation. Freud, Jung, and Nietzsche gave creators words for these tensions, and the tensions themselves (the social mask, repression, loneliness, the loss of meaning) are hardly unique to Japan.

European ideas arrive in Japan, encounter local traditions and traumas, then return to Western audiences through games and anime. Some people end up reading Nietzsche or Jung because they played _Xenogears_, _Persona_, or _NieR_ first. That is exactly what happened to me, except that I had to fly to Tokyo to close the loop. When I go back to these works now, I can see what they were doing to me back then: asking questions I didn’t know existed yet. Are we anything more than the roles others give us? Can we look at our Shadow without letting it swallow us? What is left after the gods die?

When I first played these games, I thought I was helping heroes save their world. Now I think most of them were trying to build a new one.
