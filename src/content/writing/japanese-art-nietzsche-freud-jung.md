I’ve always been a sucker for “edgy” and “pretentious” stories. Give me a tormented protagonist, some obscure religious imagery, and a plot that questions God or reality, and I’m interested. Inevitably, that led me to Xenogears (that, and my obsessions with Japan and RPGs, I guess).

I wouldn’t say the game taught me philosophy; most of its ideas went straight over my head at the time. But it made me curious. Long before I read Nietzsche, Freud, or Jung, Xenogears made me feel the questions their work would later help me understand: memory, desire, guilt, the desperate longing for meaning.

What is weird is that, at the time, I didn’t have the right words to understand what the game was depicting. I didn’t know the meaning of repression or the Jungian shadow. The quote-unquote “death of God” had no real meaning and felt like a weird paradox to me.

Yet, years later, I came across those concepts through books, and I felt a weird “anamnesis”. I wasn’t discovering them for the first time; I was coming across stuff that Japanese fiction had already exposed me to.

I believe that my story is one of many. For many people, the first exposure to Nietzsche is not through _Thus Spoke Zarathustra_, but rather in front of a God that you had to face at the end of an RPG. Freud came through a monstrous doppelgänger or a buried memory. Jung was behind a mask, a Shadow, or a persona.

But I’ve always been fascinated by how such “complex” philosophical and psychoanalytic concepts are so prevalent in Japanese works of art, whether video games, manga, or anime. And why are they SO obsessed with the same figures: the tortured teenager, the artificial code, the social mask, a falsified memory, and the end of the world?

I don’t believe this comes solely from a fascination Japanese people have with the Occident, but actually from Japan’s own traumas.
