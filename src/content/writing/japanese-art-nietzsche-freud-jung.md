I’ve always been a sucker for “edgy” and “pretentious” stories. Give me a tormented protagonist, some obscure religious imagery, and a plot that questions God or reality, and I’m interested. Inevitably, that led me to Xenogears (that, and my obsessions with Japan and RPGs, I guess).

I wouldn’t say the game taught me philosophy; most of its ideas went straight over my head at the time. But it made me curious. Long before I read Nietzsche, Freud, or Jung, Xenogears made me feel the questions their work would later help me understand: memory, desire, guilt, the desperate longing for meaning.

What is weird is that, at the time, I didn’t have the right words to understand what the game was depicting. I didn’t know the meaning of repression or the Jungian shadow. The quote-unquote “death of God” had no real meaning and felt like a weird paradox to me.

Yet, years later, I came across those concepts through books, and I felt a weird “anamnesis”. I wasn’t discovering them for the first time; I was coming across stuff that Japanese fiction had already exposed me to.

I believe that my story is one of many. For many people, the first exposure to Nietzsche is not through _Thus Spoke Zarathustra_, but rather in front of a God that you had to face at the end of an RPG. Freud came through a monstrous doppelgänger or a buried memory. Jung was behind a mask, a Shadow, or a persona.

But I’ve always been fascinated by how such “complex” philosophical and psychoanalytic concepts are so prevalent in Japanese works of art, whether video games, manga, or anime. And why are they SO obsessed with the same figures: the tortured teenager, the artificial code, the social mask, a falsified memory, and the end of the world?

I don’t believe this comes solely from a fascination Japanese people have with the Occident, but actually from Japan’s own traumas.

## A philosophy learned through images

It would be tempting to explain this phenomenon by the absence of philosophy in Japanese education. The reality is more nuanced.

Western thought has been studied in Japan since the Meiji era. Nietzsche, Freud, and Jung have been translated, commented on, and discussed in universities and intellectual circles. Thinkers such as Nishida Kitarō and the Kyoto School tried to bring European philosophy, Buddhism, and Japanese traditions into dialogue.

In high school, the subject called _rinri_, usually translated as “ethics”, can introduce the main thinkers and their doctrines. But it does not occupy the same place as philosophy in the French lycée. Students are more often asked to know concepts and authors than to construct a personal dissertation around an abstract problem.

You can therefore learn that Jung conceptualized the Shadow without ever being asked to search for what you yourself refuse to recognize. You can memorize the phrase “God is dead” without feeling what the disappearance of all the values that structured your existence would really mean.

This is precisely where fiction enters.

A class can give a definition of the unconscious. _Silent Hill 2_ places the player in an entire town shaped by the guilt and denial of its protagonist. A textbook can explain the difference between the social self and intimate desires. _Perfect Blue_ shows a young woman whose identity breaks apart under the public gaze. A teacher can introduce nihilism. _NieR: Automata_ asks the player to continue acting in a world where creators, causes, and purposes have disappeared.

Video games are, in this respect, a particular kind of philosophical laboratory. A game does more than ask: “What do you think of this idea?” It asks: “What do you do, now that this idea determines the world in which you have to act?”

This appropriation does not come only from school. Japanese creators also inherited an intellectual world shaped by the student movements of the 1960s and 1970s, the New Left, science fiction, experimental cinema, and a growing critique of institutions. Freud, Marx, Nietzsche, and existentialism circulated less as academic monuments than as tools for contesting the family, the state, work, and official narratives.

This does not mean there is a simple line between the student barricades and every contemporary role-playing game. But this counterculture helped make philosophical ideas available as artistic material. They left the libraries and entered manga, films, television series, and then video games.

## 1945: living after the sky fell

To understand why dead or monstrous gods are everywhere in Japanese pop culture, we have to return to the rupture of 1945.

The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the surrender, the American occupation, and the transformation of institutions were military and political events. They also caused the collapse of a symbolic order.

Imperial power had been placed at the center of a sacred vision of the nation. In January 1946, Emperor Hirohito’s so-called “Humanity Declaration” broke with this official conception, even if historians still debate the exact meaning of the text and the nature of imperial divinity.

It would be excessive to say that Japan literally experienced Nietzsche’s “death of God”. The European history of Christianity and the history of the Japanese imperial system are not interchangeable. But the analogy helps us understand a common experience: a world in which the highest authority can no longer guarantee values.

For Nietzsche, the death of God is not an easy victory for atheism. It is a catastrophe. If the transcendent foundation of good and truth disappears, humanity has to invent its own values—with no certainty that it will succeed.

Postwar Japan also had to rebuild a collective horizon. Economic growth, technology, the company, and consumption offered new narratives. The destroyed country became an industrial power. But this reconstruction did not erase the trauma; it covered it.

_Godzilla_, as early as 1954, gives a body to what cannot be assimilated. The creature is awakened and contaminated by nuclear power. It destroys a city that still carries the memory of the bombings. Godzilla is more than a monster: it is the return of a catastrophe that modernity claimed it could control.

In _Akira_, Tokyo has been destroyed again, then rebuilt. Under the shining metropolis remain laboratories, children sacrificed to scientific research, and a youth with no future. The technology that was supposed to make rebirth possible already contains the seeds of a new apocalypse.

This anxiety also runs through _Neon Genesis Evangelion_. The Evangelions are not simple protective robots. They are organic bodies, tied to projects that go beyond and manipulate their pilots. The adults promise to save humanity while using teenagers as instruments of their own desire for repair.

The Human Instrumentality Project promises to abolish loneliness by merging every consciousness. But this form of salvation requires the disappearance of the individual. The series then asks a basic question: should we remove the separation between human beings to end their suffering, or accept that living with others means never being able to possess them entirely?

In _Xenogears_, religions, institutions, and historical narratives also hide systems of domination. Divinity itself eventually reveals its material and constructed nature. The hero can only free himself by refusing the identities that others have made for him.

_Shin Megami Tensei_ pushes this logic even further. God does not necessarily represent Good, but absolute order. Fighting him does not automatically lead to freedom: chaos can produce another form of tyranny. Between submission to Law and the rule of force, the player has to choose—or try to invent a path that is guaranteed by no transcendence.

These works are not simply saying that God has to be killed. They distrust every system that promises total salvation. They ask what can still guide an existence when gods, parents, and institutions have failed.

## From inherited traumas to the crisis of the 1990s

Works such as _Evangelion_, _Persona_, or _Xenogears_ did not appear immediately after the war. They emerged several decades later, often in the Japan of the 1990s.

This period was marked by the collapse of the economic bubble and the beginning of the “Lost Decade”. The model based on growth, job stability, and integration into the company started to crack. In 1995, the Kobe earthquake and the sarin gas attack carried out by the Aum Shinrikyō cult increased the feeling of insecurity.

The postwar generation had believed that prosperity could replace the old order. The next generation discovered that this new promise was also fragile.

It is in this context that the teenager became a central figure. He inherits a world built by adults who ask him to save it, even though they made it uninhabitable. Shinji has to get into the Eva. Fei has to confront a history he did not choose. The protagonists of _Persona_ have to repair the corrupted desires of a society that still claims to teach them how to become adults.

Their psychological crises are therefore never entirely private. They also express the exhaustion of a collective model.

## The mask is not simply a lie

This tension between the individual and society is often analyzed through the notions of _honne_ and _tatemae_. _Honne_ refers to intimate feelings and desires; _tatemae_ to behavior adapted to social expectations.

This opposition has to be handled with care. Every society distinguishes between what we feel and what we show. Japanese people are neither without individuality nor condemned to an entirely artificial existence. The social mask is not simply a lie either: it protects privacy, limits conflict, and makes collective life possible.

It is exactly this ambiguity that makes the notion of persona so useful.

For Jung, the persona is the face we present to the world. It is not necessarily false. It becomes dangerous when we believe that it represents the whole of our being. Behind it remains the Shadow: the desires, fears, and possibilities that we refuse to recognize as our own.

The _Persona_ series directly turns this model into a narrative and gameplay system. The protagonists summon inner figures that allow them to fight. They also meet Shadows representing what they, or others, do not want to admit.

In _Persona 4_, denying your Shadow makes it more powerful. The character does not progress by destroying this embarrassing double, but by recognizing that it belongs to them. This recognition does not mean that everything the Shadow says is literally true. It means that an identity cannot be built by expelling everything that contradicts it.

In _Persona 5_, the mask takes on a more political dimension. The heroes live under the power of adults who define their place and demand obedience. Tearing off the mask becomes a painful act of insurrection. But the same mask then gives them a form, a power, and a language. It is both a prison and an instrument of emancipation.

_Perfect Blue_ explores the most violent side of this conflict. Mima leaves her career as an idol to become an actress, but the public refuses to let her change. Her former image becomes a persecuting double. She no longer knows if she really wants her new life, or if each of her identities was produced by the gaze of others.

The film does not simply oppose a “true self” to a false appearance. It asks a more disturbing question: is there still an authentic self when every way we see ourselves has been shaped by images?

## When what is buried takes a body

Freud gives another language to this crisis. For him, the repressed never completely disappears. It returns in the form of symptoms, dreams, repetitions, or behaviors whose origin the subject does not understand.

Japanese pop culture often gives a spectacular body to this return.

In _Xenogears_, Id materializes the violence tied to Fei’s trauma. His name explicitly evokes the “id”, the English name for the Freudian _ça_. But the character is not a textbook illustration of Freud. He mixes psychoanalysis, dissociation, reincarnation, and science fiction. This theoretical unfaithfulness is precisely what makes him interesting: the concept becomes a living myth.

In _Silent Hill 2_, the entire town works like a psychic space. The creatures James encounters do not simply attack him; they give a form to his guilt, his desires, and what he refuses to remember. Progressing means getting closer to a truth that the protagonist has organized his whole existence to avoid.

_Paprika_ makes the boundary even more unstable. A technology that allows people to enter dreams causes the unconscious to progressively contaminate reality. Desires are no longer locked inside the individual: they combine into a collective parade of objects, slogans, and symbols.

In _Paranoia Agent_, this dynamic becomes social. A mysterious assault gives the victims a way to escape the pressures they can no longer bear. The fantasy spreads because an entire society needs to believe in the existence of an external culprit. The collective monster is born from a shared desire not to look reality in the face.

Even a lighter work such as _Mob Psycho 100_ is based on this principle. Mob constantly holds back his emotions so that he does not hurt others. But this control turns them into a measurable pressure. When it reaches 100%, what was contained returns with uncontrollable force. Growing up then means neither repressing this force nor surrendering to it, but recognizing that emotions judged dangerous are also part of oneself.

## Playing the unconscious

Video games can go further than simply representing these conflicts, because they write them into their rules.

In _Persona_, the player divides their time between school, social relationships, and the exploration of psychic worlds. The bonds formed in daily life strengthen the abilities used in combat. The social persona and inner power are therefore not opposed: they are built together.

In _Shin Megami Tensei_, ideas become choices. Aligning with Law, Chaos, or an intermediate path changes alliances, enemies, and sometimes the world itself. The player does not watch a discussion about freedom; they have to accept the consequences of the system they choose.

_NieR: Automata_, for its part, uses repetition. Replaying some events from other perspectives progressively destroys the certainty that we understood what we were doing. Enemies become subjects. Missions lose their legitimacy. Machines imitate human behavior because they are desperately searching for a reason to exist.

The characters bear the names of Pascal, Simone de Beauvoir, or Kierkegaard, sometimes with deliberate irony. But the game does not try to teach their doctrines correctly. It turns philosophy into fragments abandoned in a world that no longer knows how to use them: return, memory, and the need for transcendence.

This appropriation is less a lesson than a creative translation. Nietzsche, Freud, and Jung become mythological material, in the same way as Kabbalah, Christianity, Buddhism, Shinto, or Gnosticism. Japan does not receive them passively.

## A collective couch, but not only a Japanese one

Calling pop culture a “collective couch” is tempting, as long as we do not reduce the whole of Japanese society to a pathology.

These works do not express a supposedly homogeneous unconscious of the Japanese people. They were produced by different creators, at different times, for audiences who do not all give them the same meaning.

They nevertheless share a group of historical tensions: the memory of war, nuclear fear, technological reconstruction, pressure from institutions, economic crisis, isolation, and the difficulty of building an identity when collective narratives collapse.

Freud, Jung, and Nietzsche give words to explore these tensions, but the tensions are not exclusively Japanese. The social mask, repression, loneliness, and loss of meaning are universal. This is why these works reached players far beyond Japan.

The cultural movement even becomes circular. European ideas arrive in Japan, meet local traditions and traumas, then return to Western audiences in the form of games and anime. Some readers then meet Nietzsche or Jung because they first played _Xenogears_, _Persona_, or _NieR_.

This is exactly what happened to me.

Coming back today to the works that marked me, I do not discover that they had secretly taught me the “correct” interpretation of Freud or Nietzsche. Their role was deeper. They had prepared me for their questions.

What speaks inside us when we lose control? Are we anything other than the roles that others assign to us? How can we live when no authority can guarantee the meaning of our existence anymore? Can we recognize our Shadow without letting it swallow us? And what remains after the death of the gods?

As a child, I thought I was helping heroes save their world. I understand today that these heroes were mostly trying to rebuild an inner world.

Long before I knew how to define the unconscious, video games had already taught me to listen to what speaks inside us when the mask begins to crack.
